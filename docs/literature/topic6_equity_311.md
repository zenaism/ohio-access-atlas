# Topic 6: Equity in pedestrian/accessibility infrastructure, 311 reporting and response, and redlining

Context: Cincinnati 311 (2004-2026; hazardous sidewalk, obstructions, curb repair, accessibility, audible signal repair) with open/close dates; city curb ramp inventory (14,970 ramps); can join ACS disability prevalence and HOLC maps. Question: would an equity analysis here be novel?

## Sidewalk / curb ramp disparities by neighborhood demographics

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed evidence that sidewalk quality is worse in lower-income and minority neighborhoods exists, but it mostly comes from single-city field audits (Pontiac) or crowdsourced audits (Project Sidewalk DC). Multi-city results are mixed. Curb-ramp-specific equity analyses are rare and mostly grey literature.

### Cited Findings
- Pontiac, MI: lower sidewalk quality was associated with lower SES and higher Black/Latinx share; ~80 miles audited 2016-2018 — [Rajaee et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8666347)
- Three-city comparison found inconsistent income/race patterns; single-city findings may not generalize — [Thornton et al. 2016](https://doaj.org/article/4c382857dde74eb78495be8a7cbab158)
- Boston: sidewalks in minority/less wealthy areas improve more slowly — [de Benedictis-Kessner (HKS)](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/where-sidewalk-ends-how-participation-contributes-inequity-basic-government-service)
- Oakland: non-compliant curb ramps and sidewalk damage are unevenly distributed, and terrain confounds the equity pattern — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9cc670pb)
- DC Project Sidewalk: more surface problems and obstacles along the historically Black Anacostia corridor (preliminary) — [Saha et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.13626)

### Inferences
- Cincinnati's hilly terrain (like Oakland's) means slope and topography should be controlled for, or apparent disparities could be confounded.
- A 14,970-ramp city-wide inventory joined to tract demographics would be larger and more complete than most curb-ramp equity analyses found.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed multi-city study of curb ramp presence or ADA compliance by race/income/disability was found.
- A blog claim that low-income areas are "40% more likely" to be out of ADA compliance (attributed to Brookings) could not be verified. Do not use it.

## 311 reporting bias and response-time inequity

### Takeaway
The literature separates two problems. (a) Reporting propensity is unequal: wealthier, whiter, more educated areas report more and faster (Kontokosta & Hong; Liu et al.). (b) Response times are only modestly unequal, and the gap is largely explained by request-type mix (Hamel & Holliday; Clark et al.). Neither problem has been studied for accessibility-specific request types.

### Cited Findings
- Kansas City 311 complaint propensity varies socio-spatially; data-driven allocation must correct for this — [Kontokosta & Hong 2021](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10314387)
- Reporting rate in NYC correlates positively with density, college share, income, and % White; up to ~3x difference across neighborhoods — [Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08620v4)
- 42M requests in 13 cities: whiter/richer neighborhoods get faster responses on average, but this is attributed to request-type composition — [Hamel & Holliday 2024](https://ideas.repec.org/a/now/jlqjps/100.00022089.html)
- 15 cities, 2007-2016: no systematic bias; varies by city; small practical differences — [Clark et al. 2020](https://researchconnect.stonybrook.edu/en/publications/do-advanced-information-technologies-produce-equitable-government/)
- Boston: 311 participation narrows the sidewalk-repair gap, but uneven participation keeps the inequity in place — [de Benedictis-Kessner](https://jdbk.scholars.harvard.edu/publication/where-sidewalk-ends-how-participation-contributes-inequity-government-service-provision)

### Inferences
- Because Hamel & Holliday attribute the gaps to request mix, Cincinnati analyses should compare response times within a single request type (e.g., hazardous sidewalk only) and model time-to-close with survival models to handle still-open tickets.
- Liu et al.'s duplicate-report method could be applied to Cincinnati sidewalk/obstruction tickets if duplicates are flagged.

### Gaps
- O'Brien et al. Boston 311 work (e.g., on coproduction/territoriality) was not verified in this session.
- No study found that focuses on 311 accessibility categories (curb ramps, audible signals) or relates response time to disability prevalence.

## HOLC redlining and present-day infrastructure

### Takeaway
Redlining is well linked to tree canopy (Locke et al.), heat, and pedestrian fatalities (Taylor et al.). No peer-reviewed study was found that directly measures sidewalk condition, curb ramps, or 311 response by HOLC grade.

### Cited Findings
- HOLC D areas have ~23% canopy vs ~43% in A, across 37 cities — [Locke et al. 2021](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/residential-housing-segregation-and-urban-tree-canopy-in-37-us-ci)
- HOLC D tracts have 2.60x the pedestrian fatality rate of A, with a dose-response; the infrastructure mechanism is hypothesized, not measured — [Taylor et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10003496/)
- HOLC polygons and census tracts rarely nest; the method for assigning grades to tracts affects results — [PLOS ONE 2022 methods paper](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9119533/)
- Scoping review: nearly all quantitative studies find redlining linked to present-day health outcomes — [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9342590)
- Grey literature: an Elmira, NY (Chemung County) report observed worse sidewalks in formerly redlined districts — [Chemung County](https://chemungcountyny.gov/DocumentCenter/View/13558)

### Inferences
- Cincinnati has HOLC maps (Mapping Inequality). A ramp-level and ticket-level point-in-polygon join avoids the tract-nesting problem.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed HOLC x sidewalk/curb ramp/311 study was found.

## Accessibility-specific (wheelchair/disability) equity analyses

### Takeaway
Disability-specific equity work is thin. Project Sidewalk produces accessibility labels and scores but has done little formal demographic equity analysis, and no study was found that joins ACS ambulatory disability prevalence to curb ramp or 311 data.

### Cited Findings
- Project Sidewalk has labeled 255,000+ accessibility problems in DC; Seattle labels were turned into neighborhood scores (0-1) — [arXiv 2502.19888](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.19888); [Saha et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.13626)
- Oakland study included ambulatory disability rates as an overlapping burden in equity-priority areas — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9cc670pb)

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that relates disability prevalence to curb ramp coverage/compliance or to accessibility 311 response times.

## Cincinnati-specific studies

### Takeaway
No academic study of Cincinnati 311 or sidewalk/curb ramp equity was found. The city's AccessCincy pages describe how accessibility requests are taken, not analyses.

### Cited Findings
- Cincinnati accepts accessibility/sidewalk reports through 311, the 311Cincy app, and online (AccessCincy) — [City of Cincinnati](https://www.cincinnati-oh.gov/manager/aging-and-accessibility/accesscincy1/); [AccessCincy Streets & Sidewalks](https://cincinnati-oh.gov/manager/aging-and-accessibility/accesscincy/accesscincy-streets-sidewalks)

### Gaps
- Only a web search was done; local theses (e.g., UC DAAP planning) or Cincinnati ADA transition plan documents were not searched.

## What remains open (novelty assessment)
1. Accessibility-specific 311 categories (curb ramps, obstructions, audible pedestrian signals) have not been studied for reporting propensity or response-time equity. The existing 311 work pools all request types or covers other services.
2. No study found joins disability prevalence (ACS) to curb ramp inventories or 311 outcomes.
3. No peer-reviewed study tests HOLC grade against sidewalk/curb ramp condition or 311 response. Taylor et al. explicitly leave the infrastructure mechanism untested.
4. Separating under-reporting from true need in accessibility complaints (e.g., Liu et al.-style duplicate methods, or comparing the ramp inventory with 311 hotspots) is unexplored.
5. Long time spans: a 22-year series (2004-2026) is longer than the panels in Hamel & Holliday (2011-2019) or Clark et al. (2007-2016) and would allow trend analysis (e.g., before/after ADA transition plan updates).
6. Rust Belt and mid-size-city evidence is limited (Pontiac only). Multi-city results are mixed (Thornton), so Cincinnati would add a new case.

Inference: a Cincinnati equity analysis that combines (a) the curb ramp inventory, (b) within-type survival analysis of accessibility 311 response times, (c) ACS disability prevalence, and (d) HOLC grades appears novel in combination, and novel in its accessibility focus. Its methods should follow Hamel & Holliday (control for request type) and Kontokosta & Hong / Liu et al. (correct for reporting bias).
